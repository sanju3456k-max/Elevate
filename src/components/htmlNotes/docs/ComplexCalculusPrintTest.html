4.1 Limits and Continuity in Euclidean Spaces $\mathbb{R}^n$

In single-variable calculus, approaching a point $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ involves exactly two directions: from the left ($x \to x_0^-$) or from the right ($x \to x_0^+$). In Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ with dimension $n \ge 2$, there exist uncountably many trajectories along continuous paths $\mathbf{r}(t)$ tending toward $\mathbf{x}_0$. Consequently, establishing the existence of a multivariable limit necessitates that the function values cluster arbitrarily close to the limit value across every conceivable continuous path.

Definition 4.1.1 (Formal $\epsilon$-$\delta$ Limit in $\mathbb{R}^n$).
Let $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $\mathbf{f}: D \to \mathbb{R}^m$. Suppose $\mathbf{x}_0$ is an accumulation point of $D$. We say that $\mathbf{L} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the limit of $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})$ as $\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{x}_0$, denoted by $\lim_{\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{x}_0} \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{L}$, if: $$\forall \varepsilon > 0, \quad \exists \delta > 0 \quad \text{such that} \quad 0 < \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0\| < \delta \implies \|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{L}\| < \varepsilon$$ where $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the Euclidean norm $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n v_i^2}$.

A central technique for demonstrating the non-existence of a multivariable limit is the Two-Path Test. If two distinct parameterizations $\mathbf{r}_1(t)$ and $\mathbf{r}_2(t)$ passing through $\mathbf{x}_0$ produce different limiting values as $t \to 0$, the limit cannot exist.

Theorem 4.1.2 (Squeeze Principle in Multivariable Domains).
Let $g(\mathbf{x}) \le f(\mathbf{x}) \le h(\mathbf{x})$ for all $\mathbf{x}$ in a punctured ball $B^*_\delta(\mathbf{x}_0)$. If: $$\lim_{\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{x}_0} g(\mathbf{x}) = \lim_{\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{x}_0} h(\mathbf{x}) = L$$ then the multivariable limit of $f(\mathbf{x})$ exists and equals $L$: $$\lim_{\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{x}_0} f(\mathbf{x}) = L$$

4.2 Total Differentiability, the Gradient Vector, and the Jacobian

The existence of all first-order partial derivatives $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}$ at a point $\mathbf{x}_0$ does not guarantee continuity, let alone differentiability. Total differentiability requires that the function admits a linear approximation whose remainder decays strictly faster than $\|\mathbf{h}\|$.

Definition 4.2.1 (Total Differentiability and the Fréchet Derivative).
A function $\mathbf{f}: U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is said to be totally differentiable at $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \operatorname{int}(U)$ if there exists a linear mapping $D\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_0) \in \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbb{R}^m)$ such that: $$\lim_{\mathbf{h} \to \mathbf{0}} \frac{\|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_0 + \mathbf{h}) - \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_0) - D\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)\mathbf{h}\|}{\|\mathbf{h}\|} = 0$$ In the standard canonical basis, the linear transformation is represented by the $m \times n$ Jacobian Matrix $J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}_0)$: $$J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\[6pt] \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_n} \\[6pt] \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\[6pt] \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}$$

When the target dimension is $m = 1$, the function is a scalar field $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$. Its derivative matrix is represented as the transposed row vector of partial derivatives, whose Euclidean dual is the Gradient Vector: $$\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}, \, \dots, \, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \right]^T \in \mathbb{R}^n$$

Theorem 4.2.2 (Directional Derivative & Steepest Ascent).
Let $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuously differentiable at $\mathbf{x}_0$. For any unit vector $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $\|\mathbf{u}\| = 1$, the directional derivative $D_{\mathbf{u}}f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ is the projection of the gradient onto $\mathbf{u}$: $$D_{\mathbf{u}}f(\mathbf{x}_0) = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) \cdot \mathbf{u} = \|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)\| \cos \theta$$ where $\theta$ is the angle between $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ and $\mathbf{u}$. Hence:
  1. The maximum rate of increase is $\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)\|$, attained when $\mathbf{u}$ is aligned with $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ ($\theta = 0$).
  2. The maximum rate of decrease is $-\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)\|$, attained when $\mathbf{u}$ is opposite to $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ ($\theta = \pi$).
  3. The directional derivative is zero along directions tangent to the level hypersurface $f(\mathbf{x}) = c$ ($\theta = \pi/2$).

4.4 Higher-Order Taylor Series and Hessian Classification

For scalar functions $f \in \mathcal{C}^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$, the multivariate Taylor series expansion about an interior anchor point $\mathbf{x}_0$ up to second order is written concisely with inner products and matrix-vector multiplications:

$$\begin{aligned} f(\mathbf{x}_0 + \mathbf{h}) &= f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)^T \mathbf{h} + \frac{1}{2!} \mathbf{h}^T H_f(\mathbf{x}_0) \mathbf{h} + R_2(\mathbf{h}) \\[8pt] &= f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x}_0)}{\partial x_i} h_i + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{\partial^2 f(\mathbf{x}_0)}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} h_i h_j + \mathcal{O}(\|\mathbf{h}\|^3) \end{aligned}$$

At a critical point $\mathbf{x}^*$, the first-order gradient vanishes identically: $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0}$. Therefore, the nature of the critical point is completely dictated by the quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{h}) = \mathbf{h}^T H_f(\mathbf{x}^*) \mathbf{h}$.

Theorem 4.4.1 (Hessian Eigenvalue Classification Criterion).
Let $f \in \mathcal{C}^2(U)$ and let $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0}$. Since $H_f(\mathbf{x}^*)$ is a real symmetric matrix by Clairaut's Schwarz Theorem ($\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_j \partial x_i}$), its eigenvalues $\{\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n\}$ are strictly real:
  • Strict Local Minimum: If all $\lambda_i > 0$ (Positive Definite, $H_f \succ 0$).
  • Strict Local Maximum: If all $\lambda_i < 0$ (Negative Definite, $H_f \prec 0$).
  • Saddle Point: If there exist $\lambda_i > 0$ and $\lambda_j < 0$ (Indefinite).
  • Inconclusive Test: If at least one $\lambda_i = 0$ and all others have identical sign (Semidefinite). Higher-order terms must be examined.

4.5 Constrained Optimization: The Method of Lagrange Multipliers

Consider the problem of optimizing an objective scalar field $f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $k$ simultaneous equality constraints: $$\text{Optimize } f(\mathbf{x}) \quad \text{subject to } \quad g_j(\mathbf{x}) = 0, \quad j = 1, 2, \dots, k \quad (k < n)$$

Theorem 4.5.1 (First-Order Necessary Conditions for Constrained Extrema).
Let $f, g_1, \dots, g_k \in \mathcal{C}^1(U)$. Let $\mathbf{x}^* \in U$ be a local extremum of $f$ on the constraint manifold $\mathcal{M} = \{\mathbf{x} \in U \mid g_j(\mathbf{x}) = 0\}$. Assume the Linear Independence Constraint Qualification (LICQ) holds: $$\operatorname{rank}\left( \begin{bmatrix} \nabla g_1(\mathbf{x}^*) & \nabla g_2(\mathbf{x}^*) & \cdots & \nabla g_k(\mathbf{x}^*) \end{bmatrix} \right) = k$$ Then there exist unique Lagrange Multipliers $\boldsymbol{\lambda}^* = (\lambda_1^*, \dots, \lambda_k^*)^T \in \mathbb{R}^k$ such that: $$\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) + \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j^* \nabla g_j(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0}$$ Equivalently, defining the Lagrangian Function $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = f(\mathbf{x}) + \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j g_j(\mathbf{x})$, the critical point $(\mathbf{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\lambda}^*)$ satisfies the unconstrained stationarity system: $$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\lambda}^*) = \mathbf{0} \iff \begin{cases} \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \mathcal{L} = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) + \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j^* \nabla g_j(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0} \\[6pt] \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}} \mathcal{L} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0} \end{cases}$$
Economic Interpretation of Multipliers: If the constraint boundary is perturbed by an infinitesimal displacement $g_j(\mathbf{x}) = c_j$, the optimal value function $f^*(\mathbf{c}) = f(\mathbf{x}^*(\mathbf{c}))$ satisfies the shadow price relationship: $$\frac{\partial f^*}{\partial c_j} = -\lambda_j^*$$ Thus, each multiplier reflects the instantaneous marginal sensitivity of the objective with respect to constraint tightening.

Problem 4.1: Path-Dependent Limit Analysis at the Origin

Investigate whether the following limit exists or diverges as $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$:

$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x^3 y}{x^6 + 2y^2}$$
Step 1: Evaluation along linear trajectories. Let $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$ along straight lines $y = mx$. Substituting gives: $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^3(mx)}{x^6 + 2(mx)^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{m x^4}{x^6 + 2m^2 x^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{m x^2}{x^4 + 2m^2} = \frac{0}{2m^2} = 0 \quad (\forall m \ne 0)$$ Along the coordinate axes $x = 0$ and $y = 0$, the function is identically zero. Hence, all straight lines yield the candidate value $L = 0$.
Step 2: Evaluation along homogeneous degree-matching parabolic curves. Observing that the denominator contains $x^6$ and $y^2 = (y)^2$, we match powers by setting $y = k x^3$. Substituting into the quotient: $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^3(k x^3)}{x^6 + 2(k x^3)^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{k x^6}{x^6 + 2k^2 x^6} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{k}{1 + 2k^2} = \frac{k}{1 + 2k^2}$$
Step 3: Synthesis and Conclusion. For $k = 1$, the path limit is $\frac{1}{3}$. For $k = -1$, the path limit is $-\frac{1}{3}$. Because the limiting value depends on the parameter $k$ of the cubic trajectory, the limit does not exist by the Two-Path Test.

Step 2: Solve the nonlinear stationary system. From (Eq. 2), we have two branching conditions: either $y = 0$ or $x = -1$.
Case A ($y = 0$): Substituting into (Eq. 1): $$6x^2 + 10x = 2x(3x + 5) = 0 \implies x = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -\frac{5}{3}$$ This produces two critical points: $P_1(0, 0)$ and $P_2\left(-\frac{5}{3}, 0\right)$.
Case B ($x = -1$): Substituting into (Eq. 1): $$6(-1)^2 + y^2 + 10(-1) = 6 + y^2 - 10 = y^2 - 4 = 0 \implies y = \pm 2$$ This produces two additional critical points: $P_3(-1, 2)$ and $P_4(-1, -2)$.

Problem 4.3: Optimal Geometry of a Cylinder Inscribed in an Ellipsoid

Determine the maximum volume of a right circular cylinder oriented parallel to the $z$-axis that can be inscribed inside the ellipsoid:

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1 \quad (a, b, c > 0)$$
Step 1: Mathematical formulation of objective and constraints. Let the cylinder have radius $r$ in the $xy$-plane and half-height $z > 0$. Then its circular boundary satisfies $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$. For maximum radius inside the elliptical cross-section $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 - \frac{z^2}{c^2}$, let $x = r \cos \theta, y = r \sin \theta$. The tightest binding radius occurs when $r^2 \left(\frac{\cos^2\theta}{a^2} + \frac{\sin^2\theta}{b^2}\right) = 1 - \frac{z^2}{c^2}$, with minimum at $\min(a, b)$.
More generally, for a rectangular prism with vertices $(\pm x, \pm y, \pm z)$, the volume is $V(x, y, z) = 8xyz$. Using the Method of Lagrange Multipliers on $g(x, y, z) = \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} - 1 = 0$: $$\mathcal{L}(x, y, z, \lambda) = 8xyz - \lambda \left( \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} - 1 \right)$$
Step 2: Gradient stationary conditions. Equating all partial derivatives to zero for strictly positive $x, y, z > 0$: $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x} = 8yz - \frac{2\lambda x}{a^2} = 0 \implies \lambda \frac{x^2}{a^2} = 4xyz$$ $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial y} = 8xz - \frac{2\lambda y}{b^2} = 0 \implies \lambda \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 4xyz$$ $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} = 8xy - \frac{2\lambda z}{c^2} = 0 \implies \lambda \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 4xyz$$
Step 3: Equality of partitioned components and final evaluation. Since all three right-hand sides equal $4xyz \ne 0$, we have: $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} = \frac{y^2}{b^2} = \frac{z^2}{c^2}$$ Substituting into the ellipsoid constraint $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1$: $$3 \left(\frac{x^2}{a^2}\right) = 1 \implies x = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}, \quad y = \frac{b}{\sqrt{3}}, \quad z = \frac{c}{\sqrt{3}}$$ The maximal inscribed volume is therefore rigorously determined as: $$V_{\max} = 8 \left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\left(\frac{b}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\left(\frac{c}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = \frac{8abc}{3\sqrt{3}}$$
